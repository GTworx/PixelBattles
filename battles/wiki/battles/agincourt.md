# Battle of Agincourt

> On St Crispin's Day, 25 October 1415, Henry V's small, sick and hungry English army, perhaps four-fifths of it longbowmen, destroyed a much larger French army in a narrow ploughed field between two woods. The French men-at-arms, funnelled into rain-soaked mud, were shot down, crushed and cut to pieces, and France lost much of its high nobility. The victory made Henry the master of Normandy, led to the Treaty of Troyes (1420), and became the defining English national legend of the war.

## Background

Henry V revived the English claim to the French crown (through Edward III) after negotiations failed in 1415. He landed in August, besieged Harfleur, and took it on 22 September, but the siege cost him dearly in dysentery and desertion. Rather than winter in Normandy or sail home, he marched roughly 9,000 men overland toward the English-held port of Calais, partly to show that he could, partly to provoke a battle with the Dauphin's party. The French, whose court was split between Armagnacs and Burgundians and whose king, Charles VI, was mentally ill, shadowed the column, broke the bridges along the Somme and finally placed themselves across the road to Calais. By 24 October the two armies were within sight of each other near Maisoncelles. The English were exhausted, short of food and ill; the French, believing they had trapped them, chose to wait for reinforcements. The Constable and Marshal Boucicaut preferred to starve the English, but the younger nobles wanted a frontal battle and prevailed.

Estimates are contested. Curry's analysis of administrative records yields only ~12,000 French against ~9,000 English, a figure most historians consider too low; contemporary chroniclers inflate French numbers much more. What every source agrees on is that the French outnumbered the English several times over in heavily armoured men.

## Terrain

The battlefield was a defile roughly 750-900 m wide between the woods of Tramecourt (west) and Azincourt (east), on a freshly ploughed field of winter wheat. Heavy rain had fallen for days and the night before, turning the field into deep, sticky mud. The woods hemmed the French in on both flanks, cutting off any outflanking manoeuvre and squeezing a larger army into a front no wider than the English could cover. The English camp lay to the south at Maisoncelles; the villages of Azincourt and Tramecourt marked the French end of the field. ## Order of battle

**English** (left to right, facing north): Camoys' left battle of men-at-arms; the king's centre battle; York's right-hand vanguard; the archers massed in wedges at both flanks and between battles, each man with a pointed stake driven in front. Men-at-arms stood perhaps four deep. The baggage and a small guard stayed at Maisoncelles to the rear.

**French**, in three "battles" about a bowshot apart and perhaps 16 ranks deep: the vanguard (~4,800 men-at-arms under d'Albret and Boucicaut), the main battle (~3,000 under Alençon and Bar) and a mounted rearguard, widely described as a leaderless dumping-ground for surplus troops. About 600 picked men-at-arms on each wing (Vendôme and Richemont) and some 800-1,200 mounted men-at-arms were assigned to break the archers. Crossbowmen and archers, supposed to open the battle, ended up crowded behind the men-at-arms and barely played a part.

## Course of the battle

1. **Stand-off in the rain (dawn to ~09:00).** Both armies deploy at dawn. The French expect reinforcements and wait; Henry cannot afford to. For roughly three hours nothing happens.
2. **The English advance (~10:30-11:00).** Henry orders the army forward, the stakes are pulled up and carried, and the line re-forms about 250-300 yards from the French at the narrowest part of the defile. The archers re-plant stakes and shoot the first volleys.
3. **The cavalry charge (~11:00).** Roughly 1,000 mounted French charge the archers on both flanks. Under strength, harried by arrows, the woods and stakes, they break; wounded horses wheel back into the advancing vanguard.
4. **The vanguard in the mud (~11:30).** The dismounted vanguard wades the ploughed field under arrow storms, heads down, exhausted by plate armour and clay, and bunches together as the woods squeeze it. It crashes into the English line.
5. **The crush (~12:00).** The line is pushed back, but the French are packed so tight many cannot fight and fall; others trip over the fallen. The archers, with arrows spent or too close for bows, drop them and attack the French flanks with swords, hatchets and mallets.
6. **The main battle joins (~12:30-13:00).** The second French line arrives and is caught in the same press. Henry fights hand to hand, protects his wounded brother Humphrey of Gloucester and takes an axe blow to his helmet; d'Albret, Alençon and the Duke of Bar are killed; York is killed on the English right.
7. **The baggage raid and the killing of the prisoners (~13:00).** Ysembart d'Azincourt and a small force plunder the English baggage. Henry, fearing the French rearguard will return and that captives may rearm, orders most prisoners killed; sources dispute exactly when the raid happened and how widely the order was carried out; Keegan argues it was meant to terrorise rather than to massacre, and the order was reportedly rescinded when the reserves fled. English knights are said to have refused to take part. The raid was probably local looting, not a serious assault.
8. **The French rearguard flees (~14:00).** The mounted rearguard leaves the field without fighting, and the battle ends.

English heralds recorded 3,069 French knights and squires dead; thousands of other bodies went unidentified. Some noble families lost every male in the line.

## Aftermath & significance

Henry marched to Calais, sailed to England on 16 November and was greeted with a triumph in London on 23 November. The French lost their leadership; the Burgundians marched on Paris within ten days and the truce between Armagnacs and Burgundians collapsed, which left Henry about 18 months to prepare a new campaign. He conquered Normandy, and the Treaty of Troyes (1420) made him regent and heir to Charles VI. English dominance lasted until Joan of Arc and the relief of Orléans (1429). Agincourt entered national memory through Shakespeare's _Henry V_ and remains the textbook case of missile troops with a good defensive position and ground that cripples an armoured charge.

## What-if levers

- **Wait for reinforcements.** The French chose to delay in the morning; had the Constable's starvation strategy held, the English would have weakened further, or the French could have attacked on better ground.
- **A wider front.** A French attack in the open field or with real flank manoeuvre would have brought their numbers to bear; instead the woods funnelled them.
- **The cavalry charge.** A properly sized and coordinated cavalry strike on the archers before the stakes were set could have broken the English line; instead it was thin, late and disorganised.
- **Crossbowmen in front.** French archers and crossbowmen positioned ahead of the men-at-arms might have disrupted the English archers.
- **The prisoners.** Without the order to kill captives, or with a French rearguard that actually attacked, the English army might have been caught between its own prisoners and a fresh enemy.
- **Dry ground.** The rain the night before was decisive; on firm ground the French advance would have been faster and less exhausting.
