# Fall of Constantinople

> After 53 days of siege, the 21-year-old Sultan Mehmed II took Constantinople on 29 May 1453 with a vast army, a fleet and the largest cannon yet used in war, ending the Byzantine Empire and the last vestige of the Roman state in the East. The city, shrunken and short of men, had held out behind its 5.7 km of Theodosian Walls for a millennium; gunpowder artillery, a fleet launched into the Golden Horn and a three-wave night assault finally defeated them.

## Background

Constantinople had been the imperial capital since 330. It had been besieged many times but taken only once, by the Fourth Crusade in 1204; the Byzantines recovered it in 1261 under the Palaiologos dynasty, but the empire declined. The Black Death (1346-49) is said to have killed almost half of the city's inhabitants, and by 1453 the city was a collection of walled villages inside walls built in the fifth century. The empire was reduced to the city, the Princes' Islands and the Peloponnese (Morea); Trebizond was independent on the Black Sea. Mehmed II became sultan in 1451 aged 19 and, in 1452, built Rumeli Hisari on the Bosporus to control sea traffic. The union of the Greek and Latin churches, agreed in 1452 as the price of Western help, was deeply unpopular in the city; the help that arrived was modest.

## Terrain

The city sits on a triangular peninsula pointing east, with the Sea of Marmara to the south, the Golden Horn to the north (Genoese Galata beyond it) and the Bosporus at its tip. The only land approach is on the west, closed by the Theodosian Walls from the Marble Tower to Blachernae: a moat over 20 m wide, a low outer wall about 8.5-9 m high and an inner wall about 12 m high with 96 towers. The walls dip into the valley of the Lycus river, the lowest point (about 35 m above sea level) and called the Mesoteichion ("middle wall"), where the moat was hard to maintain and the walls were most vulnerable. In the north the Blachernae walls, thicker but older, connect to the Golden Horn sea wall. The Horn mouth was closed by a floating chain. The map places the land walls at columns 10-12 (moat, outer wall, inner wall), with gates `g` at Charisian, St Romanus, Pegae and the Golden Gate, a rubble breach `r` at the Lycus, and Galata across the Horn.

## Order of battle

**Ottoman.** Mehmed's tent stood near the Mesoteichion with the guns and Janissaries. Karaca Pasha's European regulars faced the northern walls, Ishak Pasha's Anatolians lay south of the Lycus down to the Marmara, bashi-bazouks were behind the front, Zaganos Pasha's troops were north of the Golden Horn, and the fleet under Baltoghlu lay in the Bosporus. Orban's great bombard (reportedly hurling 270 kg stone balls, needing 60 oxen and over 400 men to move; World History Encyclopedia gives larger figures and spells the name "Urban") stood before the St Romanus Gate.

**Byzantine.** Constantine XI and Greek troops held the Mesoteichion; Giustiniani at first held the Charisian Gate and then the Mesoteichion; Minotto, with Venetians, held Blachernae; Theophilus Palaiologos and Filippo Contarini held the Pegae-to-Golden-Gate section; sea-wall detachments of Venetians, Genoese and Catalans guarded the Horn and Marmara walls; Notaras commanded a reserve at Petra; Diedo commanded the ships behind the chain.

## Course of the battle

1. **The siege opens, 2-6 April.** The Ottoman army camps outside the walls on 2 April; Mehmed arrives on 5 April and the siege proper begins on 6 April. Outlying forts at Therapia and Studius fall.
2. **The great bombardment and first assaults, 6-18 April.** The guns fire on the walls for weeks from the start of the siege (the exact start of the heavy bombardment is not settled in the sources consulted), concentrating on the Mesoteichion; Giustiniani pads the walls and fills gaps with rubble. The first Ottoman assault, on 18 April, is repulsed.
3. **Four ships break the blockade, 20 April.** Three Genoese ships and a grain ship fight through the Ottoman fleet into the Golden Horn. Admiral Suleiman Baltoghlu is wounded (reportedly in the eye); Mehmed, furious, orders him executed, but he is spared on his officers' pleas and stripped of his wealth and titles.
4. **Ships hauled over Galata, 22 April.** Mehmed has about 70 ships dragged on greased logs across the hill of Galata into the Golden Horn, outflanking the chain. A Christian fire-ship attempt on the night of 28 April fails; 40 Italians who swam ashore are impaled, and the defenders execute 260 Ottoman prisoners in reprisal.
5. **Mines, towers and a last offer, 28 April-27 May.** From mid-May the Ottomans dig tunnels under the walls; Johannes Grant, a German serving with the Genoese contingent, has counter-mines dug, and tunnels are intercepted on 16, 21, 23 and 25 May; on 23 May two captured Ottoman officers reveal the remaining mines. Scholarship also records the construction and destruction of an Ottoman siege tower, though the sources consulted do not date it. On 21 May Mehmed offers to lift the siege in exchange for the city; Constantine refuses. On 27 May a Venetian ship returns with no news of a relief fleet.
6. **First wave, 29 May, ~01:30.** Shortly after midnight the assault begins with bashi-bazouks and Christian troops in Ottoman service hitting the whole line, especially the Mesoteichion; they tire the defenders but are called off after about two hours.
7. **Second wave, ~03:30.** Anatolian regulars and azaps attack the weaker Blachernae walls and the stockade; some Turkmen briefly enter the city but are thrown back.
8. **Third wave: the Janissaries, ~05:00.** The Sultan commits his Janissaries; Giustiniani is badly wounded and leaves the wall, which panics the defenders. Ulubatli Hasan is said to have led the way onto the stockade. According to Doukas, the postern called the Kerkoporta was accidentally left open, letting in about fifty Turks who raised their banner on the inner wall; excavations found no matching gate in the inner wall, so the story may be legend.
9. **The city falls, dawn of 29 May to 1 June.** Ottoman troops enter by the breach and the postern, Constantine dies fighting (accounts vary, see below), the defence disintegrates and the city is plundered for three days.

## Outcome & casualties

|                   | Ottoman                          | Byzantine                                                               |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Killed            | unknown, believed severe         | ~4,000 killed (Nicolle); Emperor Constantine XI dead                    |
| Captured/enslaved | none                             | ~30,000 enslaved (Crowley); up to 60,000 by Leonard of Chios            |
| Notes             | Mehmed made the city his capital | ~550+ Venetian dead; Giustiniani died on 1 June and was buried on Chios |

Constantine's death is uncertain: the popular account has him throwing aside his purple and charging into the fighting near the Gate of St Romanus; Barbaro has him hanging himself; some Ottoman sources have him killed in a chance encounter with marines farther south. A severed head presented to Mehmed may or may not have been his. Giustiniani was accused by Venetian writers of feigning his wound; Genoa denied the charge.

## Aftermath & significance

Mehmed moved the capital from Adrianople to Constantinople and (reportedly) wept at the sight of the ruin. The Byzantine Empire ended; remnants in the Morea and Trebizond fell within a decade. The fall is often used to mark the end of the Middle Ages, and it demonstrated that gunpowder artillery had made even the greatest medieval walls vulnerable.

## What-if levers

- **A relief fleet.** Venice's fleet reached the city in time on 27 May only as a scouting ship; a real relief might have broken the blockade.
- **The Golden Horn.** Had the ships not been hauled over Galata, the defenders could have kept the Horn walls thinly manned.
- **Giustiniani's wound.** His removal at the key moment collapsed the defence of the stockade; had he stayed, the Janissary wave might have been repulsed.
- **The Kerkoporta.** If Doukas is right, a single unbarred postern let the Ottomans in behind the stockade; the story is disputed.
- **Notaras and the Genoese of Galata.** More support from the Greek aristocracy or Galata could have lengthened the siege.
- **Bombard reliability.** Orban's gun reportedly broke up after about six weeks; a failure earlier, or a slower Ottoman gun supply, could have made the walls hold longer.
