# Battle of Waterloo

> On Sunday 18 June 1815, Napoleon's Armée du Nord attacked the Duke of Wellington's Anglo-allied army on the Mont-Saint-Jean ridge south of Waterloo. Wellington's [[reverse-slope-defence]] and [[infantry-square]]s held against French artillery, infantry and cavalry until Blücher's Prussians, arriving on Napoleon's right, forced the emperor to divert his reserves and the repulse of the [[imperial-guard]] broke the French army. The defeat ended the Hundred Days and Napoleon's rule.

## Background

Napoleon abdicated in April 1814 and was exiled to Elba. He returned to France in March 1815 and retook power without a fight ("the Hundred Days"); the Allies declared him an outlaw and assembled armies. Napoleon's plan was to strike first in Belgium, before the Duke of Wellington's Anglo-allied army and Field Marshal Blücher's Prussians could unite, and defeat each in turn.

The French crossed the frontier near Charleroi on 15 June. On **16 June** Napoleon beat Blücher at Ligny while Marshal Ney's force fought Wellington's troops to a draw at Quatre Bras. On **17 June** the Prussians withdrew north to Wavre rather than east, and Wellington fell back to the ridge of Mont-Saint-Jean. Napoleon detached about 33,000 men under Marshal [[emmanuel-de-grouchy]] to pursue the Prussians, and followed Wellington with the rest. Wellington chose to give battle on the 18th only after Blücher assured him of Prussian support. Heavy rain on the night of 17–18 June soaked the ground, and Napoleon delayed his attack to let it firm up for his artillery.

## Terrain

The battlefield was a compact area, about 4 km wide, crossed by the paved Brussels–Charleroi road running north to south. Wellington held the **Mont-Saint-Jean ridge**, a low east–west escarpment along the sunken Ohain road, and placed most of his infantry just behind the crest, hidden from French guns. In front of the ridge he held three strongpoints: the walled chateau of **Hougoumont** on the west, the farm of **La Haye Sainte** beside the road (about 400 men of the King's German Legion, with riflemen in a sand quarry opposite), and the hamlets of **Papelotte** and La Haye on the east. A shallow valley of rye and clay, soaked by rain, divided the armies, and the French held the facing ridge around the inn of **La Belle Alliance** and the farm of Rossomme. **Plancenoit** and the **Bois de Paris** lay on the French right rear, toward which the Prussians marched from Wavre via the Lasne valley. The Forêt de Soignes lay behind Wellington. On the scenario map one tile is roughly 140 m.

## Order of battle

- **French (left to right):** Reille's II Corps (about 13,000 foot, including Prince Jérôme's division that opened the attack on Hougoumont) on the west; d'Erlon's I Corps (about 16,000 foot) on the east; Lobau's VI Corps, the Imperial Guard and the cavalry reserve (including Milhaud's and Kellermann's cavalry) in the centre and rear; a grande batterie of about 80 guns in front of d'Erlon's corps.
- **Allies (right to left):** Hougoumont anchoring the right (garrisoned under Lt-Col James Macdonell by light companies of the Coldstream, Third and First Guards, plus Nassau troops and Hanoverian detachments); Maitland's Guards, Colborne's 52nd and Chassé's Dutch-Belgian division on the right-centre; the centre behind La Haye Sainte (held by about 400 men of the King's German Legion's 2nd Light Battalion under Major Georg Baring); Picton's 5th Division with Kempt's and Pack's brigades and Bylandt's Dutch-Belgians on the left-centre; Papelotte on the left; the Earl of Uxbridge's cavalry, including the Household and Union Brigades, behind the line.

## Course of the battle

1. **Deployment (about 06:00–11:00).** The armies deploy after a rain-soaked night. Napoleon waits for the ground to harden, while Wellington's infantry lies behind the crest. Grouchy is at Wavre, 20 km east.
2. **Hougoumont diversion (about 11:30).** Jérôme's division and later more of Reille's corps attack the chateau as a feint to draw Wellington's reserves. A French soldier breaks the north gate open with an axe, but Macdonell and a few men shut it again, trapping and killing those who entered (a drummer boy was spared); the garrison holds all day, and Wellington later said the battle turned on the closing of the gates; later French howitzer fire burns much of the chateau without taking it. (Wellington recorded the opening nearer 10:00; sources differ on timings.)
3. **Grande batterie and d'Erlon's attack (about 13:00–13:30).** The big battery bombards the left-centre, then four divisions of I Corps attack in dense columns. Bylandt's Dutch-Belgians give way; Kempt's and Pack's brigades and Picton's division stop the columns at the hedge, and Picton is killed. At about 13:15 Napoleon sees Prussian columns at Lasne-Chapelle-Saint-Lambert, far off to the east.
4. **The Household and Union cavalry charge (about 14:00).** Uxbridge's heavy cavalry smash into d'Erlon's columns and capture two eagles (the Scots Greys' Sergeant Ewart took that of the 45th Line), but pursue into the grande batterie and are ridden down by French lancers and cuirassiers; the Union Brigade loses its commander, Ponsonby. About 3,000 of I Corps are lost, and the corps needs until about 16:00 to re-form.
5. **Ney's cavalry against the squares (about 15:30–16:00).** Napoleon orders Ney to take La Haye Sainte at about 15:30, but Ney, taking Allied movements to the rear for the start of a retreat, launches the cavalry reserve (about 9,000 horse in total) against the ridge. Sources place the first charge between about 15:30 and 16:00. The Allied infantry form [[infantry-square]]s between the batteries; the gunners shelter in them between charges. The French cavalry, unsupported by infantry or guns, cannot break a square and is largely spent.
6. **Prussians at Plancenoit (about 16:30–18:00).** Bülow's IV Corps attacks Lobau's flank and Plancenoit. Napoleon commits about 10,000 men, among them the Young Guard and then Old Guard battalions, to hold the village, which changes hands repeatedly, and so does not have the infantry to pursue his centre breakthrough.
7. **La Haye Sainte falls (about 18:00).** The KGL garrison runs out of ammunition and is overrun. French skirmishers and guns now fire from close range into the Allied centre, which is badly thinned. Zieten's Prussian I Corps reaches the Allied left after General Müffling persuades him to support Wellington rather than march on to Bülow.
8. **The attack of the Imperial Guard (about 19:00–19:30).** Napoleon sends the Imperial Guard against the Allied right-centre (one source counts about 14 battalions, close to 4,000 men, advancing in two columns; accounts of the numbers differ). Maitland's Guards, the 52nd Foot under Colborne and Chassé's Dutch division break the attack at close range. The Guard recoils, a cry of "La Garde recule" goes through the army, and Wellington signals a general advance.
9. **General advance and rout (about 20:00–21:30).** The Allied army advances; Zieten's men break the French right near Papelotte and the Prussians take Plancenoit for the last time. Old Guard squares south of La Haye Sainte and around La Belle Alliance cover the retreat. Wellington and Blücher meet near the inn and the Prussians pursue through the night.

## Outcome & casualties

| Force        | Casualties (killed, wounded, missing)                                                                                                                                       |
| ------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| French       | ~25,000–33,000 killed or wounded depending on source, plus ~7,000–8,000 captured (the National Army Museum gives nearly 40,000 killed, wounded or captured); ~220 guns lost |
| Anglo-allied | ~17,000 (about 3,500 killed, 10,200 wounded, 3,300 missing); one source puts Allied losses at about 24,000, apparently including the Prussians                              |
| Prussians    | ~6,600–7,000                                                                                                                                                                |

The sources agree on the broad figures but differ on exact counts and on how deserters are treated; many modern accounts add that thousands of French soldiers simply deserted. At least 7,000 horses were also killed or wounded.

## Aftermath & significance

Napoleon returned to Paris and abdicated on 22 June; the Allies entered Paris on 7 July, restoring Louis XVIII, and Napoleon was exiled to Saint Helena, where he died in 1821. Waterloo ended the Hundred Days and the First French Empire, and opened a long period of relative European peace under the Congress system. Wellington called it "the nearest-run thing you ever saw in your life."

## What-if levers

- **Grouchy's march.** Had he marched toward the cannon at Wavre or kept contact with Blücher's main body, he might have delayed or intercepted the Prussians.
- **Start time.** Beginning the attack at 09:00 rather than ~11:30 would have given Napoleon several more hours before the Prussians arrived.
- **Hougoumont.** Treating the chateau as a pure diversion, rather than feeding in more of Reille's corps, would have saved infantry for the centre.
- **Ney and the cavalry.** Coordinated cavalry, infantry and artillery against Wellington's centre after La Haye Sainte fell might have broken it, instead of unsupported charges at squares.
- **La Haye Sainte.** Taking it earlier, or reinforcing its garrison with ammunition on the Allied side, would have changed the centre's strength.
- **The Guard.** Committing the Old Guard to the centre instead of Plancenoit, or delaying the Guard's attack until the Prussians were contained.
