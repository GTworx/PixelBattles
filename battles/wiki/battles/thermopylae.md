# Battle of Thermopylae

> In 480 BCE the Spartan king [[leonidas-i]] led a small allied Greek army to hold the coastal pass of Thermopylae against [[xerxes-i]]'s huge invasion force, while the Greek fleet fought the Persian navy at Artemisium. For two days a few thousand hoplites stopped the Persians in the narrows. When [[ephialtes-of-trachis]] showed the Persians the Anopaea mountain track, Leonidas sent most of his army away and stayed with the Spartans, Thespians and Thebans to die. It was a Persian tactical victory, but the stand became the great symbol of Greek resistance.

## Background

After Marathon ([[marathon]]), [[darius-i]] prepared a second invasion but died in 486 BCE. His son Xerxes I assembled an army and fleet, bridged the Hellespont, and dug a canal through the Athos peninsula. Under the Hellenic League formed in 481 the Greeks planned to meet him at two linked chokepoints: the narrow pass of Thermopylae on land and the straits of Artemisium off northern Euboea at sea. An earlier plan to hold the Vale of Tempe was dropped when the army learned that the Persians could bypass it. Sparta, because of the Carneia festival (and the Olympic truce), sent only a vanguard under one of its two kings, with Leonidas bringing the 300 men of his bodyguard, whose fathers were living so that no family line would be extinguished.

## Terrain

The pass ("Hot Gates", from its sulphur springs) lay between the Kallidromon massif and the Malian Gulf. In 480 BCE the sea washed the foot of the cliffs; today alluvium has pushed the shore several kilometres out. At its narrowest, the Middle Gate, the pass was about 100 m wide or less, probably wider elsewhere. The Phocians had built a defensive wall across it. West of the pass lay Trachis and the Persian camp; east lay the road to Locris, the East Gate and Alpenus. A hill ("Kolonos"), where excavators found Persian arrowheads in 1939, rises just behind the wall. High above, the Anopaea path climbed from the Asopus gorge, ran along the ridge through oak woods, and descended to the East Gate behind the Greek position. The scenario map shows the sea on the north, the mountain wall on the south, the wall at the Middle Gate with a gap, and the track through the mountains (1 tile about 60 m; the gate is widened for play).

## Order of battle

The Greeks held the Middle Gate with the Spartans at the sharp end, Peloponnesians and Locrians in relays behind, and Thespians and Thebans with them; Herodotus has the contingents rotating in and out. 1,000 Phocians were posted on the Anopaea path. The Persians stood west: archers and the Medes and Cissians in the van, the Immortals in the second wave, the huge imperial levies and the cavalry behind in the wider ground by Trachis. The scenario scales the Persian army to about 94,000, with 60,000 of them levies, which is inside the modern range.

## Course of the battle

1. **The pass is held; Xerxes waits (the days before).** Leonidas takes the Middle Gate and repairs the wall. Xerxes waits four days at Trachis, hoping the Greeks will go, and rebuffs Leonidas's "Molon labe" ("come and take them") by attacking.
2. **Day 1 morning.** Persian archers shoot, then Medes and Cissians attack frontally, ordered to bring the Greeks alive. They fail in the narrow front: long spears and tight shield formation outmatch them.
3. **Day 1 afternoon.** The Immortals attack and are also beaten. Herodotus reports that the Spartans used a feigned retreat and turn, and rotated units by city.
4. **Day 2.** More waves attack in contingents. Xerxes, perplexed, withdraws. That evening [[ephialtes-of-trachis]] betrays the Anopaea path to the king (Livius finds the story hard to credit, since Persian scouts could have found the path themselves, and the exact route is uncertain).
5. **Night of Day 2.** [[hydarnes]] with the Immortals (Diodorus says 20,000 men) climbs the path from the Asopus gorge by night.
6. **Day 3 dawn.** The Immortals surprise the Phocians, who retire onto a hill; the Persians ignore them and hurry down. Leonidas holds a council and dismisses most of the allies; the 300 Spartans, the 700 Thespians (who refuse to leave) and the 400 Thebans stay, roughly 1,500-2,000 men in all. The Acarnanian seer Megistias, who had joined of his own accord, sent his son away but stayed himself.
7. **Day 3 morning.** The Greeks advance into the wider ground and kill many. [[leonidas-i]] falls and the Greeks fight four times over his body, according to Herodotus; Herodotus names two brothers of Xerxes, Abrocomes and Hyperanthes, among the Persian dead. Livius calls the four rescues "too homeric to be true" and notes that those nearest Leonidas left no survivors to tell it.
8. **Day 3 afternoon.** Surrounded, the survivors retire behind the wall to the hill of Kolonos and are killed by arrows and spears. The Thebans move apart and surrender with hands raised (a few are killed first) and are later branded with the royal mark; Xerxes had Leonidas's head put on a stake (World History Encyclopedia).

The Persians forced the pass: a Persian tactical victory.

## Aftermath & significance

At Artemisium the Greek fleet, hearing that the pass had fallen, withdrew to Salamis after an indecisive struggle and storms that wrecked many Persian ships. Boeotia submitted and Athens was evacuated and burnt. The Greeks then won at Salamis (480) and Plataea (479), ending Xerxes's invasion. The battle has been cited ever since as a model of terrain and discipline as force multipliers. Leonidas's bones were returned to Sparta about forty years later, and a stone bearing Simonides's epitaph ("Go tell the Spartans...") stands at the site.

## What-if levers

1. **Phocians on the path.** If Leonidas had posted a stronger or more reliable force on Anopaea, or fortified the summit, the flanking march might have failed.
2. **Ephialtes's betrayal.** Without the traitor the Persians might have found the track later or not at all.
3. **Staying in strength.** If the whole allied army stayed, they would have been encircled and lost, so the decision to dismiss them preserved the army for Salamis.
4. **Spartan reinforcement.** If Sparta had sent its full army instead of waiting for the Carneia, a defence of the pass might have lasted longer.
5. **Artemisium and weather.** If the fleet had held longer, a Persian army cut off from supplies might have run short.
