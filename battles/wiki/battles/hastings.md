# Battle of Hastings

> On Saturday 14 October 1066 William, Duke of Normandy, defeated King Harold II of England on the Senlac ridge, a few miles from Hastings. An English army of foot soldiers standing in a [[shield-wall]] on a hill held out for most of a day against Norman archers, infantry and [[heavy-cavalry]]. It broke only after repeated [[feigned-retreat]]s thinned it and Harold, with two of his brothers, was killed. The result began the Norman Conquest.

## Background

King Edward the Confessor died on 5 January 1066 without an heir. The Witan chose Harold Godwinson, the most powerful earl in England, and he was crowned at once. Two rivals denied his claim. William of Normandy said Edward had promised him the throne and that Harold had sworn to support him. Harald Hardrada of Norway rested his claim on an earlier agreement between the Norwegian and English crowns, and he was joined by Harold's exiled brother Tostig.

Harold spent the summer with troops and a fleet on the south coast. He dismissed much of that force around 8 September when supplies and the harvest season ran out. Hardrada and Tostig landed in the north and beat the northern earls at Fulford on 20 September. Harold marched north and destroyed them at Stamford Bridge on 25 September. William, delayed by contrary winds, landed unopposed at Pevensey on 28 September, built a castle at Hastings and began ravaging the surrounding country, which was Harold's own family land. Harold marched south, about 240 miles by one count, and camped on Caldbec Hill on the night of 13 October. William moved out of Hastings the next morning.

## Terrain

The battlefield lies on the Senlac ridge, between Caldbec Hill to the north (where Harold's column halted) and Telham Hill to the south (from which the Normans came). The English stood at the crest of a steep slope, in a dense line, with woods and marshy ground protecting the flanks and the ground in front falling toward a stream and marsh at the foot. The London-Hastings road ran over the ridge. The slope is gentler today because abbey building levelled the ridge top, so the modern ground understates how hard the climb was. Some scholars place the English line at Caldbec Hill instead of the abbey ridge, and the historic debate over the exact site means the map is schematic. World History Encyclopedia describes the English position as a low "hammer-head" ridge with woods on the flanks and a stream and marsh in front. The scenario map draws the ridge across the middle of the map with woods on both flanks, marshy ground and streams at its foot to the west and east, Telham Hill at the bottom and the Malfosse ditch (location disputed) in the north-east.

## Order of battle

**English (north, on the ridge, facing south).** A close-packed shield wall the length of the crest. The housecarls and the royal standards (the Dragon of Wessex and the king's own Fighting Man) stood at the centre around Harold, with the fyrd on either side; the brothers Gyrth and Leofwine were with the king. Some skirmishers with javelins and stones stood in front, and part of the levy was kept behind the line. The scenario shows six units: housecarls (2,000), western and eastern fyrd (1,800 each), the Gyrth-Leofwine contingent (1,000), skirmishers (600) and a rear reserve (800).

**Normans (south, at the foot of the slope, facing north).** Three divisions, left to right: Bretons, Angevins, Poitevins and Maine men under [[alan-the-red]]; Normans under William in the centre; French, Picards, Flemings and Boulonnais under William FitzOsbern and [[eustace-ii-of-boulogne]] on the right. Archers stood in front, infantry behind them and the mounted knights in reserve. The scenario shows archers (1,500), the three infantry divisions (2,000, 2,200, 1,800) and three cavalry groups (1,000 with William, 700 each wing).

## Course of the battle

1. **Deployment on Senlac Hill (dawn to about 09:00).** Harold holds the crest; William's army comes up from Hastings over Telham Hill and forms in three divisions below the English line.
2. **Archers and infantry assault uphill (about 09:00).** Norman archers shoot uphill with little effect; arrows glance off shields or pass over. William sends in his spearmen, who face javelins, spears, axes and stones and cannot break the wall.
3. **Cavalry repulsed; the Breton line breaks (late morning).** The cavalry attack also fails. The Bretons on William's left give way and flee, and a rumour spreads that the duke is dead.
4. **William lifts his helm; the pursuers are cut down (around midday).** Some English leave the line to chase the Bretons, possibly led by Gyrth and Leofwine, both of whom are killed. William rides along his line bareheaded to show he lives and turns the cavalry on the isolated pursuers. Whether Harold ordered the pursuit or it was spontaneous is unclear; Wace says he told his men to hold, which no other account confirms. Lawson argues from the Bayeux Tapestry's hillock scene that English light infantry may have been on that high ground from the start and not caught while chasing, so the order of events here follows the traditional reading and is contested.
5. **Lull and the first feigned retreat (early afternoon).** After a pause for rest and food, William tries cavalry assaults that wheel away as if beaten and turn on the English who follow. William of Poitiers says the manoeuvre was used twice. Historians debate whether it was real or a later embellishment, though most accept it was used.
6. **Arrows and cavalry wear the wall down (afternoon).** A second feigned flight draws out more levies. The housecarls thin out and are replaced by fyrd troops. The line contracts around the standard but holds.
7. **Harold is killed (dusk, about 17:00).** Sunset was about 16:54 and the field was nearly dark by 18:00. Accounts of the king's death conflict. The Bayeux Tapestry shows a man with an arrow in the eye next to a man struck by a sword, under a caption announcing that Harold has been killed, and it is unclear which figure is the king. Amatus of Montecassino is the earliest written source for the arrow. William of Malmesbury and Wace repeat the arrow; Malmesbury adds a knight who wounded him at the same moment. The Carmen de Hastingae Proelio credits William, which is generally doubted. The Battle Abbey chronicle says no one knew who killed him. The household troops fought on around the body. World History Encyclopedia gives the traditional version as an arrow in the eye, then a cavalry charge and sword blows, and John of Worcester places his death around twilight after a full day's fighting. The sources are too contradictory for certainty.
8. **English collapse and the Malfosse (dusk to nightfall).** With the king dead most of the army fled and the Normans pursued. At the Malfosse ("Evil Ditch"), a ditch or earthwork whose exact location is unknown (World History Encyclopedia calls it a hill; the Battle Abbey chronicle gives no location; Wace has Normans dying in a ditch they had crossed earlier), English fugitives turned and fought and wounded Eustace of Boulogne before being overrun.

## Aftermath & significance

Harold's body was identified (legend says Edith the Fair recognised it from marks only she knew, and that Harold's mother offered its weight in gold, which William refused). The English proclaimed Edgar Ætheling king, but William advanced on London and was crowned at Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day 1066. Resistance continued for years: risings in 1067-1068, the Harrying of the North of 1069-1070 and Hereward the Wake's revolt at Ely in 1070. William founded Battle Abbey on the site, its high altar traditionally marking where Harold fell. The Bayeux Tapestry, probably commissioned by [[odo-of-bayeux]], records the story, and many dispossessed English later served in the [[varangian-guard]] in Constantinople.

## What-if levers

- **Harold's army in September.** Dismissing the fyrd and fleet on 8 September left him to fight two invasions in three weeks with a tired army.
- **Haste.** Harold marched south days after Stamford Bridge instead of gathering more levies in London. A delay might have denied William the chance to fight on ground that suited him.
- **The pursuit.** If the English line had stayed closed when the Bretons broke, the Norman left might not have rallied.
- **No feigned retreat.** If the English had ignored the feints, William would have been left to attack uphill with archers and cavalry that had already failed.
- **William's survival.** The rumour of his death at midday nearly decided the day; had he not shown his face, the Norman army might have dissolved.
- **Harold's death.** An English line that did not lose its king at dusk might have held until night.
