# Battle of Stalingrad

> Fought between 23 August 1942 and 2 February 1943, Stalingrad was the largest and deadliest urban battle in military history and the turning point of the war in Europe. German 6th Army was drawn into a grinding street fight for the city on the Volga while its long flanks, held by weaker Romanian armies, were struck by [[operation-uranus]]; about a quarter of a million Axis troops were encircled ([[encirclement]]), the relief attempt failed, and the survivors surrendered.

## Background

In the summer of 1942 Germany's offensive in southern Russia aimed at the Caucasus oil fields; the 6th Army under [[friedrich-paulus]] and the 4th Panzer Army under [[hermann-hoth]] were directed toward Stalingrad. On 23 July Hitler widened the campaign to include occupation of the city, which carried Stalin's name and was an industrial centre and a transport hub on the Volga's river route. By late July the Don and Volga were only about 65 km apart at their closest. The first clashes occurred on 17 July at the Don approaches; on 1 August [[andrey-yeryomenko]] took command of the Southeastern Front; on 23 August the 6th Army and 4th Panzer Army attacked, supported by heavy bombing from Luftflotte 4 under von Richthofen.

## Terrain

Stalingrad was a long, narrow city, some 40 km from north to south and often only a few kilometres wide, strung along the high west bank of the Volga, which was too wide to bridge and with the German front pressed against it. From north to south the major landmarks were the suburbs of Rynok and Spartanovka, the **Tractor Factory**, the **Barrikady** (Red Barricades) gun factory, the **Red October** steel works, the hill of **Mamayev Kurgan** (Height 102) that commanded the city, the central railway station, the Zaritsa ravine, the grain elevator and Pavlov's House, and the southern districts toward Beketovka. The east bank was held by Soviet artillery and was the route for supplies and reinforcements crossing by night. West of the city lay the open Don–Volga steppe, with the Don bend about 65 km west near Kalach. Winter brought temperatures far below freezing. ## Order of battle

- **Soviet:** the 62nd Army in the city centre and factories, the 64th Army to its south at Beketovka, heavy artillery and rocket units on the east bank, then the counteroffensive forces: Southwestern Front (5th Tank, 21st Armies) from the north-west, the Don Front to the north, and the Stalingrad Front (51st and 57th Armies with the 4th and 13th Mechanized Corps) from the south. One source puts the Red Army force in the city in September at about 54,000 men; no source consulted gives later totals, so the scenario's later 62nd Army figures are illustrative.
- **Axis:** 6th Army's divisions in the city and on its northern and western flanks, XIV and XXIV Panzer Corps, 4th Panzer Army to the south, the Romanian Third Army along the Don to the north-west and the Romanian Fourth Army and VI Corps to the south, and later LVII Panzer Corps for the relief attempt.

## Course of the battle

1. **Drive to the Volga and the bombing (23–25 August 1942).** The 6th Army and 4th Panzer Army attack on 23 August while about 600 Axis aircraft bomb the largely wooden city over 23–26 August, killing many civilians. Axis troops reach the outer limits of the city on 3 September and enter it on 12 September. Soviet guns on the east bank and the 62nd and 64th Armies hold the outskirts.
2. **Street fighting (12 September – early October).** The 62nd Army under Chuikov (formed in the city on about 11 September) holds it street by street. Mamayev Kurgan changes hands so often that attackers and defenders are hard to tell apart. The grain elevator is held by about fifty men for five days, and Pavlov's House by a platoon for about two months. Close-range fighting negates German air and armour advantages ([[urban-warfare]]).
3. **The factory district (October).** The 14 October assault falls on the Tractor Factory, Barrikady and Red October; fighting in the Barrikady district continues until late October, and the Soviets hold on to a strip along the river.
4. **Last German push (mid-November).** By mid-November the defenders are pressed into narrow zones along the west bank; the fighting centres on "Lyudnikov's Island". The 6th Army is exhausted, and its flanks rely on Romanian, Italian and Hungarian troops while Stavka masses reserves.
5. **Operation Uranus (19–21 November).** On 19 November the Southwestern and Don Fronts attack the Romanian Third Army, which is overrun; the Stalingrad Front strikes the Romanian Fourth Army on 20 November. The Romanian line, thin, under-equipped and outnumbered, collapses ([[operation-uranus]], [[combined-arms]]).
6. **The ring closes (22–23 November).** Soviet forces cross the Don and take Kalach on 22 November, and the two pincers meet there on 23 November. Paulus estimates about 250,000 men inside the pocket (the _Kessel_, see [[encirclement]]). Hitler forbids a breakout and Göring promises an airlift of 300 tons a day; it delivers about 90 (about 120 in January).
7. **Operation Winter Storm (12–23 December).** Manstein's LVII Panzer Corps, with 4th Panzer Army support, attacks from Kotelnikovo. It is weak at the start (the 23rd Panzer Division had no more than thirty tanks). By 19 December it is within about 48 km of the pocket, with the 6th Panzer Division across the Myshkova, but the Soviet 2nd Guards Army (about 90,000 men) has been moved up and Operation Little Saturn threatens Manstein's flank. The 6th Army, with fuel for only about 30 km and no authority from Hitler, does not break out. Manstein abandons the attempt on 23 December (one source says 28 December).
8. **Operation Ring (24 December – 26 January).** The airlift delivers a fraction of the 750–800 tons Paulus estimated he needed and the pocket starves. On 10 January Rokossovsky's Don Front opens Operation Ring behind nearly 7,000 guns and mortars. Pitomnik airfield falls on 16 January and Gumrak around 22 January; on 26 January the 21st Army links up with the 13th Guards Division north of Mamayev Kurgan and cuts the pocket in two. Soviet surrender terms (7–8 and 22–24 January, sources differ) are rejected by Hitler.
9. **Capitulation (27 January – 2 February).** The pocket splits again; Paulus, promoted field marshal on 30 January, surrenders on 31 January, and General Strecker ends resistance in the northern pocket on 2 February. About 91,000 prisoners, including 22 generals, go into captivity; only about 5,000 return (the Operation Koltso article's infobox gives 107,800 captured).

## Aftermath & significance

The battle ended Germany's 1942 summer offensive, removed Axis forces from the Caucasus, and passed the strategic initiative to the Red Army for the rest of the war. It was the first time a Hitler-led field army surrendered, and Russia commemorates 2 February among its Days of Military Honour. It is widely regarded as the pivotal battle of the European theatre.

## What-if levers

- **Campaign priorities.** Concentrating on the Caucasus or on the Don rather than splitting effort between the oil fields and the city.
- **The flanks.** Replacing Romanian armies with German reserves behind the Don, or withdrawing the 6th Army from the Volga early in November.
- **The breakout.** A break-out on 22–24 November (when Paulus asked) or in December when Winter Storm was within 48 km.
- **The airlift.** Göring promised 300 tons a day against Paulus's estimated need of 750–800, and delivered about 90; a different supply plan might have prolonged the pocket.
- **Chuikov's hold.** The 62nd Army's survival on a strip of the river bank was far from certain in October and November.
- **Soviet timing.** An earlier Uranus, or a different Winter Storm defence, would have changed the encirclement.
