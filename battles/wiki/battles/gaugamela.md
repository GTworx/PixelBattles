# Battle of Gaugamela

> On 1 October 331 BCE [[alexander-the-great]], leading roughly 47,000 men, defeated the far larger army of [[darius-iii]] on a plain the Persians had levelled for their [[scythed-chariots]]. By edging his army to the right and drawing the Persian left away from the centre, Alexander opened a gap, drove his [[companion-cavalry]] through it towards the king, and broke the Achaemenid army. It was the second and final pitched meeting of the two kings, and the practical end of the Persian Empire.

## Background

After Issus (333 BCE) Alexander declined Darius's offers of ransom and territory and spent two years taking the Levantine coast, Egypt and Tyre. In 331 he marched east from Egypt, crossed the Tigris, and found that Darius, who had gathered troops from across his empire, was waiting on the plains north-east of Nineveh. Darius had learned from Issus, where the narrow ground had cancelled his numerical advantage: he chose open country and had it cleared and levelled so that his scythed chariots and cavalry could manoeuvre. (Wikipedia; World History Encyclopedia.)

Alexander scouted the Persian position, and World History Encyclopedia reports that a captured advance party revealed Darius's numbers and the traps prepared in front of his line. In Arrian's account [[parmenion]] urged a night attack, and Alexander refused, arguing that victory should be won openly in daylight and not stolen; Arrian adds that Darius kept his army standing to arms all night, fearing exactly that. (Diodorus is said to report that Alexander took the advice, so the anecdote is contested.) A lunar eclipse of 20–21 September 331 BCE, which Alexander's seer Aristander read as favourable, falls shortly before the battle and underlies the traditional date.

Ancient numbers are not credible: Arrian gives 40,000 cavalry and 1,000,000 infantry, Diodorus 200,000 and 800,000, Curtius 45,000 and 200,000. The Macedonian figure (Arrian: 7,000 horse and 40,000 foot) is generally accepted. The scenario uses a mid-range Persian army of about 80,000.

## Terrain

An open, essentially flat plain on the Nineveh Plains, north of the Jebel Maqlub ridge, with the village of Gaugamela on the Bumodus (Khazir) river and Arbela to the south-east. The exact site is disputed; Tell Gomel, Karamlesh, Qaraqosh, Tell Aswad and a mound near Wardak have all been proposed, with Karamlesh the most widely accepted. The Persians cleared vegetation and levelled the ground in front of their line for the chariots, and Alexander's army was able to move over the prepared area; on his right, though, the ground the Persians had not prepared was rougher, which helped to blunt the chariots and stretch the Persian cavalry. The Persian line outflanked the Macedonian line by more than a mile. The map places the Bumodus along the north, Jebel Maqlub's hills at the far west, and Tell Gomel in the north-east.

## Order of battle

Left to right as each army faced the other.

**Macedonian and Greek line (south, facing north).**

- Left: Parmenion, with Thessalian cavalry (under Philip), Greek allied cavalry, Thracian cavalry and Achaean and Cretan infantry; the left was to hold.
- Centre: the phalanx in brigades (Simmias and Coenus are named in Wikipedia; Craterus appears in Warfare History Network), with the hypaspists (Nicanor, in Arrian) on its right.
- Right: Alexander with the Companion cavalry (Arrian names Philotas as commanding the Companions), Paionian light horse, Prodromoi (Aretes), Agrianians, javelin-men and archers; mercenary cavalry in angled flank guards.
- Rear: a second line of allied and mercenary infantry ordered to turn about and meet any encirclement, giving the whole force a double front.

**Persian line (north, facing south).**

- Left (opposite Alexander): Bessus with Bactrian, Dahae, Arachosian, Persian, Susian and Scythian cavalry, with the chariots forward of the left and centre-left.
- Left-centre: Indian cavalry, Mardian archers and the Persian foot guard (Immortals).
- Centre: Darius with the Royal Kinsmen, Greek mercenaries, Carian infantry and household cavalry, backed by the levies. The 15 Indian elephants appear to have stayed with the camp: Arrian counts them among the Persian forces, World History Encyclopedia and Warfare History Network say they were not used, and TheCollector says Parmenion later captured nearly a dozen with the baggage (see [[war-elephants]]).
- Right (opposite Parmenion): Mazaeus with Syrian, Median, Mesopotamian, Parthian and other cavalry; Armenian and Cappadocian horse in advance, and Albanian and Sacesinian horse sent to circle the Macedonian left.

## Course of the battle

1. **Deployment on the levelled plain (dawn).** Both armies form up. The Persians have been standing to arms through the night. Alexander's order: a hollow oblong, flank guards angled back, a second line facing the rear. Outnumbered in cavalry by more than five to one, he cannot allow himself to be surrounded.
2. **Oblique advance to the right.** Alexander advances and edges steadily right, away from the cleared ground. Darius, fearing he will be drawn onto rougher terrain, orders Bessus's cavalry to shadow the Macedonian drift and extend leftward.
3. **Cavalry clash and the chariot charge.** Scythians and Bactrians open the fight on the Macedonian right; Arrian describes a long and hard-fought cavalry action in which the Greeks were often hard-pressed. Darius launches the 200 scythed chariots. Agrianian javelin-men and archers cut down teams, the phalanx opens ranks to let the rest through, and hypaspists and grooms dispatch them. The elephants are never committed.
4. **A gap opens in the Persian line.** Darius feeds more horse to his left to halt the drift; Persian pressure on Parmenion builds. The Persian formation stretches until a gap opens between its left and its centre, and Simmias's section of the phalanx, engaged on its own front, lags behind the advance, opening a second gap on the Macedonian side.
5. **The Companion wedge drives at Darius.** Alexander wheels the Companions into a wedge, with the nearest phalanx behind, and drives into the gap straight at the king. The Royal Kinsmen and Greek mercenaries are broken after fierce fighting.
6. **Darius flees.** As the wedge nears, Darius turns his chariot and flees; the Persian centre and left disintegrate. The legendary spear-throw that kills the king's charioteer comes from this phase.
7. **Parmenion under pressure; the camp raid.** On the left Mazaeus's cavalry press Parmenion hard and send for Alexander. Through the gap behind Simmias, Persian and Indian horse ride to the Macedonian camp (Warfare History Network adds 2,000 Cadusians and 1,000 Scythians sent round the flank by Mazaeus; TheCollector says they also looked for the captured Persian queen mother), but plunder rather than attack the phalanx from the rear; the second line turns about and destroys many of them.
8. **Alexander turns back; Mazaeus breaks.** Alexander abandons the pursuit and rides to help the left. On the way he meets fleeing Parthians, Indians and Persians and fights what Arrian calls the fiercest cavalry fight of the day, losing about sixty Companions. Mazaeus, seeing the king gone, disengages; the Thessalians counter-charge and the Persian right collapses. The pursuit runs through the night and Parmenion captures the Persian camp, baggage and elephants.

Times are not reliably recorded; the sources give an order of events rather than a clock.

Alexander won decisively. Darius escaped, according to Arrian towards Media after Alexander's pursuit reached Arbela; the army dissolved as a fighting force and the Persian camp, the king's chariot and bow, the elephants and about 4,000 talents were captured.

## Aftermath & significance

Alexander took Babylon (Wikipedia credits Bagophanes with surrendering it) and then the other Persian capitals. Darius fled east and in 330 BCE was seized and murdered by [[bessus]], satrap of Bactria, who proclaimed himself king. Alexander buried Darius with royal honours and later captured and executed Bessus. The traditional end date of the Achaemenid Empire is Darius's death. Tactically, Gaugamela showed that disciplined combined arms, a flexible reserve and decisive leadership could defeat numbers many times larger on ground the enemy chose.

## What-if levers

1. **Night attack.** Had Alexander taken Parmenion's advice, his army would have been in disorder against an enemy that was awake and ready; or conversely might have caught Darius while the lines were forming.
2. **Chariots on prepared ground.** If the Persians had been able to run the chariots on levelled ground along the whole front, rather than on the part Alexander avoided, the phalanx might have been broken.
3. **Bessus reinforces too far.** Had Darius held the centre and let the left cavalry deal with Alexander alone, no gap would have opened.
4. **Darius holds his ground.** Darius's flight at the critical moment decided the army's collapse; a king who stood might have allowed Mazaeus's success on the left to turn the battle.
5. **The camp raid turns the right way.** The Persian cavalry that broke through to the camp could have struck the phalanx from the rear instead of looting.
6. **Alexander pursues, not turns back.** Chasing Darius immediately might have ended the war at once, but could have left Parmenion to be destroyed.
