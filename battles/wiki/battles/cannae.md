# Battle of Cannae

> On 2 August 216 BCE [[hannibal-barca]], with perhaps 40,000–50,000 men, destroyed a Roman and allied army of about 80,000 on the plain of Apulia. By bowing his centre forward and letting it give ground while his African infantry and cavalry closed on the flanks and rear, he produced the textbook [[double-envelopment]]. It was the worst single-day defeat in Roman history, and the model of a battle of annihilation for every later staff college.

## Background

Hannibal crossed the Alps in 218 BCE and beat Roman armies at the Trebia (218) and Lake Trasimene (217). The Romans then adopted the delaying strategy of Fabius Maximus, which angered many; for 216 the Senate elected two consuls, the cautious [[lucius-aemilius-paullus]] and the aggressive [[gaius-terentius-varro]], and raised an unprecedented eight legions with equal numbers of allies. In the spring Hannibal seized the Roman supply depot at Cannae, which forced the consuls to march south and fight. Polybius says the consuls took chief command on alternate days; on Varro's day he ordered an advance over his colleague's objections, leading to a skirmish that favoured Hannibal. Livy has Varro in command on the day of the battle and forcing it over Paullus's objections.

The armies camped about 8 km (some sources say closer) apart. After a Roman success in a skirmish gave Varro confidence, Paullus put two-thirds of the army in a main camp east of the Aufidus and a smaller force across the river; Hannibal offered battle on 1 August, which Paullus declined, and Carthaginian cavalry harassed the Roman water parties.

## Terrain

Open ground on the left (south) bank of the Aufidus (modern Ofanto) in Apulia, with the village and hill of Cannae behind the Carthaginians. The river anchored the Roman right and Hannibal's left, so neither side could be turned on that side; the Roman left, on the open plain, was the only line of retreat. In Polybius's account the Romans faced south and the Carthaginians north, so the rising sun troubled neither side, and the passage as translated does not mention wind. The Wikipedia article reports low sun and a south-easterly Volturnus wind blowing dust at the Romans; treat that tradition as uncertain. The Roman main camp lay to the north-east, the smaller one across the river.

## Order of battle

Left to right as each army faced the other. The map places the Romans in the north, facing south, and Carthage in the south facing north, so each army's "right" is on the screen's left.

**Roman line.**

- Left, on the open plain: Varro with allied cavalry (about 4,000).
- Centre: the heavy infantry, drawn up deeper than normal, with the principes immediately behind the hastati; Servilius Geminus.
- Right, on the river: Paullus with Roman citizen cavalry (about 2,400).
- Velites in front; the camp garrisons behind.

**Carthaginian line.**

- Left, on the Aufidus: Hasdrubal with ~6,500 Iberian and Gallic heavy cavalry.
- Centre: Gallic and Iberian infantry bowed forward in a crescent, Hannibal and Mago in front, slingers and light troops screening.
- Wings of the crescent: African (Libyan) heavy infantry, held back as a reserve.
- Right, on the open plain: Hanno with ~3,500–4,000 Numidian light cavalry.

## Course of the battle

1. **Crescent deployment (morning).** The armies form up, the Romans facing south. Hannibal's convex centre faces the Roman massed column; the Libyans, who in Polybius carry Roman equipment taken as spoils in earlier battles, stand back at each end.
2. **Skirmishers and the cavalry clash at the river.** Light troops fight indecisively; then Hasdrubal's heavy cavalry crashes into the Roman citizen cavalry on the narrow ground between the river and the infantry. The Romans are driven back and many fight on foot; the wing is destroyed.
3. **The Roman centre pushes in.** The legions advance on the Gallic and Iberian centre. The Celts and Iberians give ground slowly, step by step.
4. **The crescent inverts.** The convex line is bent back to a concave pocket, and the Roman mass, losing its frontage, crowds into the gap. Meanwhile the Libyans on each wing stand unengaged, flanking the funnel.
5. **The African infantry close the flanks.** On a signal the Libyans wheel inward, striking the right and left Roman flanks; the centre turns and holds. The Romans are attacked on three sides.
6. **Hasdrubal sweeps round the Roman rear.** Having beaten the Roman right-wing cavalry, Hasdrubal rides behind the Roman army to the left, where he and the Numidians rout the allied cavalry; Varro flees with a small escort to Venusia.
7. **Encirclement.** Hasdrubal's cavalry attacks the Roman rear. The ring is complete, and the Romans are so closely packed that they cannot fight effectively.
8. **Annihilation.** Slaughter continues until nightfall. Paullus, wounded earlier, is killed; so are Servilius Geminus and Minucius Rufus.
9. **The camp garrison is taken.** The 10,000 men Paullus left to guard his camp (in Polybius, meant to attack Hannibal's camp during the battle) are shut in by Hannibal's relief force and captured. Separately, Livy (22.51) has Maharbal urging Hannibal to march on Rome and Hannibal refusing; see Aftermath on how doubtful that exchange is.

Times are only approximate; the sources describe an afternoon battle, with the sun, wind and dust deployed from morning.

## Aftermath & significance

Rome fell into panic, consulted the Sibylline Books, practised human sacrifice (burying living people in the Forum Boarium), enlisted slaves, debtors and criminals and rejected Hannibal's peace offer. Capua, Tarentum and many southern Italian communities defected; Philip V of Macedon pledged support, beginning the First Macedonian War, and Syracuse changed sides. Hannibal did not march on Rome: In Livy (22.51) Maharbal urges an immediate march and remarks that Hannibal knows how to win a victory but not how to use one; Polybius never mentions Maharbal at Cannae, the passage comes from a poorly transmitted manuscript, and the anecdote is widely regarded as doubtful. Delbrück argued the army was too weak for a siege, and Hannibal's aim was to break the Roman alliance. The war ran another 14 years, ending at Zama in 202 BCE. Rome responded by avoiding pitched battle, adopting more flexible formations and more unified command.

## What-if levers

1. **Paullus's caution wins.** Had Paullus refused battle on the open plain Varro favoured, Hannibal would have faced the Fabian strategy and starvation.
2. **The wind and sun.** A deployment that kept the dust at Hannibal's front rather than the Romans' could have blunted the crescent.
3. **Reserves left behind.** Using the 10,000 men in the camps as a reserve, or to attack the Carthaginian camp, might have drawn Hannibal off.
4. **Roman cavalry on the river flank.** If the citizen cavalry had held Hasdrubal off for even an hour, the encirclement could not have been closed.
5. **Wheeling the Libyans too soon.** Had the Roman centre broken Hannibal's line before the Libyans swung in, the Carthaginians might have been split.
6. **March on Rome.** Had Hannibal taken Maharbal's advice immediately, Rome might have been forced to terms or been given time to prepare.
