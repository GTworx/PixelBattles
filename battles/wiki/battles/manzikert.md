# Battle of Manzikert

> On 26 August 1071 the mobile Seljuk army of Sultan Alp Arslan destroyed the field army of the Byzantine emperor Romanos IV Diogenes near Manzikert in the Armenian borderlands. Seljuk [[horse-archers]] spent the afternoon avoiding a decisive fight. A misread order to return to camp at dusk, the withdrawal of the rearguard under [[andronikos-doukas]] and a Seljuk counter-attack turned the day into a rout. The emperor was captured, the first Byzantine emperor taken by a Muslim commander.

## Background

By the 1060s Byzantium's eastern defences had decayed. The Iberian army had been disbanded around 1053, the army relied on mercenaries and conscripts, and Constantine X Doukas had favoured the civil service over the military. Seljuk Turks raided Anatolia repeatedly. Romanos, a Cappadocian general who became emperor in 1068 by marrying the dowager empress Eudokia, campaigned against them with limited success and clashed with the Doukas family. His reforms were unpopular with the nobility and the troops.

In 1071 Alp Arslan was besieging Aleppo and Romanos marched into Armenia to retake fortresses before the sultan could react. The march was long and strained relations in the army: Romanos's baggage train was luxurious, his Frankish mercenaries plundered locals, and his generals disagreed on whether to press on or fortify. Alp Arslan was nearer than the emperor believed and had good intelligence.

The scenario shows about 37,000 Seljuks (33,000 mounted) and about 22,800 Byzantines, reflecting a field army weakened by detachments and defections. This split is a modelling choice and not a reliable count.

## Terrain

Manzikert (modern Malazgirt) lies on the upland plain north of Lake Van in the Iberia theme, in country of open grassland and farmland ringed by low hills. The Byzantine army had marched from Theodosiopolis (Erzurum) via Hinis, about 230 km, with a detachment sent toward Khliat (Akhlat). The sources give almost no topographic detail for the fighting itself, and the exact site is not firmly identified; the scenario map is therefore schematic. It shows the small fortress town of Manzikert and the Byzantine camp at the western edge, a stream running north-south near it, a broad plain in the middle, hills north and south where the Seljuk wings could shelter and ambush, and the Seljuk camp at the east. The Seljuks formed a crescent about four kilometres from the Byzantines, with its horns in the hills.

## Order of battle

**Byzantines (west).** Romanos in the centre with the Varangians and the main body of tagmata and household; Nikephoros Bryennios on the left; Theodore Alyates on the right; Andronikos Doukas commanding the rearguard and reserve behind the line. Archers and light troops screened the front.

**Seljuks (east).** A wide crescent with horse archers in the centre and on both horns, the horns lodged in the hills, the sultan's ghulams behind the centre, and a reserve and camp guard to the rear. The units in the scenario are the centre (10,000), two horns (7,000 each), the ghulams (4,000), a reserve (5,000) and the camp guard of Kurds and Turkmen (4,000).

## Course of the battle

1. **The Byzantines leave camp (morning, 26 August).** After Romanos rejects Alp Arslan's peace overture on 25 August, the army marches out in order. This follows days of skirmishing: on 23 August Romanos took Manzikert; on 24 August a foraging force under Bryennios met the Seljuk main body and withdrew and a cavalry force under Basilakes was routed, Basilakes being captured; on 25 August Turkic mercenaries defected and Uzes deserted.
2. **The advance across the plain (midday).** The Byzantine line advances. The Seljuk centre falls back, shooting as it goes, while the horns harass the wings.
3. **Harassment and the stretched wings (afternoon).** The Seljuks keep withdrawing and harrying. The Byzantine wings try to envelop the crescent and are drawn away from the centre and the rearguard, which lags behind. The army is tired as the day lengthens.
4. **Alp Arslan's empty camp (late afternoon).** By late afternoon the Byzantines have overrun the sultan's camp, but the Seljuk cavalry has avoided pitched combat throughout. Romanos is a long way from his own camp with dusk coming.
5. **Romanos orders the return to camp (dusk).** The emperor orders a withdrawal. The right wing misreads it, and the Seljuks fall on the turning line.
6. **Doukas withdraws the rearguard.** Andronikos Doukas does not cover the retreat; he marches back toward the camp, and in some accounts says the emperor has been cut down. The sources disagree on motive. Attaleiates, who was in the camp and did not see the fighting, says "many report" that someone spread false news of the emperor's death, and Skylitzes and Zonaras treat it as betrayal; Bryennios the Younger blames the rearguard's failure to support the emperor without alleging treason. The Medievalists.net author argues the rearguard had drifted too far back to intervene and that the treason charge shifted blame from Romanos. Doukas's family later benefitted from Romanos's fall.
7. **The Seljuk counter-attack (twilight).** The right wing breaks, then the left. The army dissolves into groups hunted down in the dusk.
8. **Romanos is captured (night).** The centre and the Varangians are encircled. Romanos is wounded, his horse killed, and he is taken prisoner. By dawn the professional core of the army has been destroyed.

The timing of the "return to camp" order and the exact sequence of the collapse differ between Attaleiates (an eyewitness), Psellos, Scylitzes and the Muslim sources; the phases above follow the broad consensus in secondary accounts. A 2026 Medievalists.net article dates the counter-attack to shortly after the afternoon prayer, reads the order as the imperial standard being turned, and notes that some flank units took this for the collapse of the centre or the emperor's death.

Alp Arslan at first did not believe the bloodied prisoner was the emperor. After confirming his identity he had Romanos made to submit (Psellos reports a boot on the neck) and then treated him courteously. After about a week Romanos was released on terms: the cession of Antioch, Edessa, Hierapolis and Manzikert, a marriage alliance, and a ransom of 1.5 million gold pieces plus 360,000 a year (reports differ on details).

## Aftermath & significance

Andronikos Doukas reached Constantinople and had his cousin Michael VII proclaimed emperor. Romanos was defeated in Cilicia, surrendered at Adana and was blinded at Kotyaion on 29 June 1072; he died soon after of infection. Provincial commanders competed for the throne, and the civil wars and economic crisis weakened resistance in Anatolia. The Seljuks did not immediately pursue or reoccupy the region, and Alp Arslan died in November 1072, but Turkmen settlement spread and by about 1080 large areas had been lost; the Sultanate of Rum was established at Nicaea around 1078. Alexios I Komnenos restored order after 1081, and the empire's appeal for help later fed into the First Crusade. Historians disagree about the weight of the battle: Norwich calls it the empire's greatest disaster, while Delbruck argues its significance was overstated.

## What-if levers

- **Splitting the army.** Detaching Tarchaneiotes's force before the battle took perhaps half the army away and nothing is known for sure of its fate.
- **Refusing the truce.** Romanos rejected Alp Arslan's peace overture on 25 August.
- **Not advancing so far.** A fortified camp and a shorter advance would have kept the army close to the rearguard.
- **Timing the retreat.** Withdrawing at dusk with the line stretched invited the Seljuk counter-attack.
- **Doukas's rearguard.** If it had covered the retreat, the Byzantine centre might have escaped intact.
- **The emperor's exposure.** Romanos fought in the front line and was captured, which turned a defeat into a political collapse.
