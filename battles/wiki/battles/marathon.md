# Battle of Marathon

> In September 490 BCE a Persian amphibious force sent by Darius I to punish Athens landed on the Bay of Marathon, 40 km from the city. A much smaller army of Athenians and Plataeans, led in the field by [[miltiades]] and the polemarch [[callimachus]], charged across the plain, broke both Persian wings, then turned inward on the Persian centre. The victory ended the first Persian invasion, became the founding myth of classical Athens, and showed that disciplined [[hoplite-phalanx]] heavy infantry could beat the Persian army's lighter troops on suitable ground.

## Background

Athens had supported the Ionian Revolt (499-493 BCE) against Persian rule, helping to burn Sardis in 498. After crushing the revolt, [[darius-i]] resolved to punish Athens and Eretria and to bring the Aegean under control. A first expedition under Mardonius (492) was wrecked by a storm off Mount Athos. In 490 Darius sent a seaborne force under the Median admiral [[datis]] and [[artaphernes]], son of the satrap of Sardis. It took the Cycladic islands, then sacked Eretria on Euboea after a short siege. The exiled Athenian tyrant Hippias accompanied the fleet and advised landing at Marathon, ground suited to the Persian cavalry and close to his old supporters. Athens had earlier executed Persian envoys, and now sent the runner Pheidippides to Sparta for help. Sparta was celebrating the Carneia festival and promised to march only after the full moon.

Herodotus gives no army size for either side. The size and role of the Persian cavalry is one of the great open questions of the battle (see Course).

## Terrain

The plain of Marathon is a crescent of flat ground about 10 km long running along a shallow bay facing east. The coast is lined with a sandy beach. To the north-east lay the Great Marsh (Schinias) and to the south the smaller Brexiza marshland; both were boggy and are where many fleeing Persians died. West and north the plain is closed by the slopes of Mount Agrieliki and the Penteli range. The Athenians camped on a spur of Mount Agrieliki beside a sanctuary of Heracles, their flanks protected by a grove or abatis of felled trees (Herodotus is ambiguous), and thus blocked the two roads leading out of the plain towards Athens (the south road via Pallene/Vrana and the north-east road). The Persian beach camp stood near the shore. ## Order of battle

Reading left to right from the Greek side (north to south), the Athenian line stood at the foot of the hills facing east:

- **Left wing:** Plataeans (~1,000), then the Athenian tribes forming the left, eight ranks deep.
- **Centre:** the tribes Leontis (under Themistocles) and Antiochis (under Aristides), thinned to about four ranks to lengthen the line.
- **Right wing:** the Athenian tribes forming the right, eight deep, under [[callimachus]] by custom of the polemarch holding the right.

The Persians faced west with their best troops, the Persians and Sakae, in the centre, and levy infantry from the empire on the wings; archers stood behind the front rank. The Persian cavalry is not mentioned fighting; many historians argue it was away foraging, watering, or already re-embarking, which would explain why the Athenians dared to attack. The fleet lay drawn up on the beach.

## Course of the battle

1. **Landing and stalemate (days 1-5 before the battle).** The Persians land on Hippias's advice. The Athenians march out and occupy the exit routes. Pheidippides goes to Sparta; the Plataeans arrive. Both sides wait about five days. Persian reluctance to attack uphill and the Athenian wait for Spartan help are the standard explanations.
2. **The line forms (dawn, 12 September).** The strategoi are said to have been split five-five on whether to fight; Miltiades urged Callimachus, whose casting vote it was, to attack. The line is extended to match the Persian front by weakening the centre.
3. **The run (morning).** Advancing in good order until within bowshot (Herodotus says eight stades, roughly 1.5 km, though modern writers consider it shorter), the Greeks broke into a run to cut the time under arrow fire. Herodotus says it was the first Greek charge at the run; modern scholars doubt a full-armour sprint over a mile and argue for a fast advance.
4. **Clash (mid-morning).** The strong Persian centre drove the thin Athenian centre back, but on both wings the Greeks overwhelmed the Asiatic levies. Callimachus fell on the right, with the strategos Stesilaus.
5. **Double envelopment (late morning).** The victorious wings did not pursue but, as Herodotus says, "drew together" and attacked the Persian centre from both flanks while the centre stood to fight again.
6. **Rout to the ships (midday).** The Persians fled to the shore; some were pushed into the marsh. At the shore Cynegeirus (brother of the playwright Aeschylus) seized a Persian trireme, had his hand cut off and died; seven ships were captured and the rest got away.
7. **Race to Athens (afternoon-evening).** In Herodotus's account the fleet sailed round Cape Sounion for Athens (Livius says Phaleron, and some modern historians place this move before the battle, with the cavalry re-embarking for it). The Athenian army marched the roughly 40 km back in heavy armour and was in position by late afternoon, so the Persians turned back to Asia. The central tribes stayed to guard the field under Aristides. The Spartans arrived the day after the battle, having covered about 220 km in three days, toured the field and agreed the Athenians had won; their numbers are not given in the sources I consulted.

## Outcome & casualties

| Side             | Killed                         | Notes                                                                                                           |
| ---------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Athens & Plataea | 192 Athenians (Herodotus)      | Buried in the _soros_, the burial mound still on the plain; Plataean losses unrecorded                          |
| Persia           | 6,400 on the field (Herodotus) | More drowned in the marsh; 7 ships captured; modern estimates of total dead are sometimes 1,000-2,000 or higher |

The result was a clear Greek victory and the end of the 490 expedition. The persistent figure of 6,400 to 192 is probably exaggerated, but the lopsided ratio is accepted.

## Aftermath & significance

Athens was saved and its democracy vindicated; the veterans, the "Marathonomachai", became a civic ideal. Darius died in 486 and his son [[xerxes-i]] returned in 480 on a far larger scale (see [[thermopylae]]). Miltiades led a failed expedition to Paros in 489 and died in disgrace. The battle also showed the potential of [[hoplite]] infantry against the Persian arrow-and-wicker-shield style, and the long-term Athenian rise of naval power was driven by Themistocles after Marathon. The "marathon" race legend, in which Pheidippides runs from Marathon to Athens, is a later tradition (Plutarch, Lucian) and not in Herodotus.

## What-if levers

1. **Cavalry on the field.** If the Persian cavalry had been present and engaged, the Greek charge across open ground might have been broken.
2. **Waiting for Sparta.** Had Miltiades not forced the decision, the Athenians might have waited several more days while Persian numbers and a Hippian faction inside Athens grew.
3. **Pursuit.** If the Greek wings had pursued the routed levies rather than turning in, the Persian centre would have retired intact to the ships.
4. **Callimachus's vote.** The polemarch's casting vote split the council; a different vote means no battle.
5. **Persian landing at Phaleron.** If the fleet had reached Athens before the army, the city, with its pro-Persian faction, might have opened its gates.
