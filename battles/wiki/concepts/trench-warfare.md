# Trench warfare

## Definition

Fighting from lines of dug-in, connected trenches protected by barbed wire, machine guns and artillery, in which the defender holds a large advantage over an attacker crossing open ground.

## How it worked

By the end of 1914 magazine rifles, machine guns and quick-firing artillery made movement in the open so costly that both armies on the Western Front dug in, and the line ran unbroken from the North Sea to Switzerland. A typical system had a front line, support and reserve trenches joined by communication trenches, with wire belts in front and deep dugouts behind; from 1916 the Germans built defence in depth, a thinly held forward zone and a battle zone of strongpoints behind it, with counter-attack divisions in reserve. Attackers tried long bombardments (a week before the Somme), gas from 1915, mining, tanks from 1916 and, by 1917–18, short surprise bombardments with predicted fire and [[infiltration-tactics]]. The defender could usually bring up reserves by rail faster than the attacker could move forward over the shelled ground, which is why breakthroughs before 1918 stalled.

## Where it appears

- [[world-war-i]]: the Western Front from late 1914 to the spring of 1918 (Ypres, Verdun, the Somme, Passchendaele), Gallipoli, the Isonzo and the Macedonian front; on the Eastern Front the lines were longer and thinner and more often broken.
