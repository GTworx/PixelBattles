# Galley warfare

## Definition

Naval warfare with galleys: long, low warships driven mainly by oars, which dominated the Mediterranean until the late sixteenth century.

## How it worked

Oars let a galley move against the wind or in a calm, when a sailing ship lay helpless. Sixteenth-century galleys carried heavy guns in the bow, aimed by turning the whole ship, and won mainly by boarding with their large crews: opposing lines met head on and locked bow to bow. Galleys were poor in rough weather and on the open sea, and needed nearby bases for water and supplies, which made sheltered harbours and friendly coasts vital. Full-rigged sailing ships with many guns along their sides could beat off galleys if they had wind enough to manoeuvre, and in time this made the galley obsolete; hybrids such as the Venetian galleass tried to combine the two.

## Where it appears

- [[preveza]]: Barbarossa's galleys waited in the shelter of an Ottoman gulf, then, when the wind died on 28 September 1538, rowed against the Holy League's becalmed carracks and galleons. The Venetian galleon's heavy guns held them off, a lesson that pushed Western shipbuilding toward the galleass.
